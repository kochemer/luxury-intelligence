    AI is Supercharging Identity-Driven Cyber Threats in Retail

    By Ryan Ikeler
    Facebook Facebook Twitter Twitter LinkedIn LinkedIn Email Email

    When retail executives think about cybersecurity, minds jump to classic nightmare scenarios: blacked-out register screens during peak holiday shopping, supply chain halts at distribution centers, or encrypted point-of-sale (POS) databases. But retail’s threat landscape has fundamentally evolved.

    According to IBM, the average cost of a data breach in the retail industry rose to $3.8 million in 2026, up from $3.54 million the year before, as attackers increasingly rely on impersonation tactics to conduct high-damage data breaches. The same report finds that voice and SMS phishing were used in 17 percent of breaches, carrying the highest average cost of any attack vector, at $5.29 million.

    As threat groups like ShinyHunters have demonstrated, breaking into complex retail networks is no longer necessary when attackers can simply get legitimate passwords reset and log in. By targeting single sign-on (SSO) portals, identity providers, and cloud management platforms, intruders walk right through the front door. Today, artificial intelligence (AI) is making these identity-based attacks faster, cheaper, and more believable.

    How Vishing Exploits Retail’s Identity Sprawl

    Retailers operate under unique identity constraints: seasonal hiring spikes, high store associate turnover, and thousands of deskless employees accessing portals across hundreds of locations. Vishing (voice phishing) thrives in this environment. An attacker impersonates a regional director, calls the helpdesk that supports hundreds of stores during a weekend rush, and talks a stretched, unfamiliar-with-every-caller team into an urgent credential reset or multifactor authentication (MFA) override. That approach still works well enough that most attackers haven’t needed to change. However, a handful of reported cases have used real-time voice cloning to make the same call even more convincing. Retailers should treat that as a trend worth watching.

    Because a helpdesk-approved reset or MFA override creates an authorized session, legacy security checks fail to flag the intrusion. Attackers increasingly pivot from compromised credentials to interconnected software-as-a-service (SaaS) and on-premises corporate systems, silently exfiltrating high-value retail assets like bulk customer personally identifiable information (PII) and loyalty databases. They then extort the retailer, even contacting affected customers directly to pressure payment.

    Rethinking the Crisis Response

    Most retailers’ incident response plans traditionally focused on business continuity: restoring from backup and minimizing downtime. In an identity intrusion, there’s nothing to restore. Systems operate normally while threat actors hide inside authorized sessions and hold exfiltrated data for ransom. Responding effectively means focusing the first critical hours on identity containment and eviction.

    Response teams must take immediate containment steps, including changing passwords, revoking active sessions, revoking persistent OAuth tokens, and locking down cloud platforms and SSO tenants. Rebuilding authentication trust and restricting broad application permissions between store-level systems and corporate data are essential, but they only close the door attackers have already walked through. The stronger move is closing the social engineering vectors that let them in: hardening helpdesks against vishing, manually or cloned, with strict out-of-band verification, and moving to phishing-resistant MFA rather than SMS or voice codes.

    The strongest position, though, isn't reactive. The retailers pulling ahead are the ones drawing on visibility into how these intrusions unfold, from which credentials get targeted first to which vectors keep resurfacing case after case, and using that to prioritize their defenses around what's already being exploited elsewhere rather than waiting to discover it firsthand. Retailers that make that shift, from responding to a known playbook to anticipating the next one, are the ones positioned to grow through the next disruption.

    The Bottom Line

    For retailers, customer trust and brand reputation are the ultimate currency. As cybercriminals use AI to bypass traditional perimeters, protecting that trust requires retiring outdated ransomware playbooks. Forward-looking retailers are doing more than hardening their identity layer against today's threats. They're building the kind of proactive culture that turns security from a defensive cost into a competitive advantage.

    Ryan Ikeler is the president of MOXFIVE, a leader in cybersecurity incident response helping organizations respond to incidents and minimize the risk of future attacks.
